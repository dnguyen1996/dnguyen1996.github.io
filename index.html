I build repeatable systems out of ambiguous processes on engineering teams. Each project here is told as a case study.

What a case study covers

Every case study follows the same shape, so you can compare projects and jump to what matters to you.

  • SnapshotCompany, my role, the team, how long it ran and who was affected.
  • The problemWhat was broken, who it hurt and why it had to change.
  • What made it hardThe one or two real constraints, such as a fixed date or competing teams.
  • Key decisionsTwo or three calls I made. Each lists the options, what I chose, why, who pushed back and how it turned out.
  • How it ranThe phases and how long each took, plus what went wrong and how I adjusted.
  • OutcomeWhat shipped and what happened afterward.
  • What I’d do differentlyOne or two specific changes I would make next time.
  • ArtifactsSimplified versions of documents I used to run the project, such as a roadmap, a scope table and a risk log.

Questions each one answers

  • How do I handle a fixed date and more requests than the team can build?
  • What did I give up, and what did that cost?
  • Who disagreed with me, and how did we settle it?
  • How did it turn out, including the parts that went wrong?
  • What would I do differently?

How to read them

  • I use “I” for what I did and “we” for what the team did.
  • They are written in plain language. Internal code names, customer names and exact financials are left out.
  • The documents under Artifacts are recreated for this portfolio. They are not the originals.
Activision · Technical Project Manager

From a tangled ban system to one service five teams use

Player Flags MVP10 months13 people across five teams
Company
Activision
Role
Technical Project Manager
Core team
3 engineers, 1 engineering manager, and me as TPM
Partner teams
A UI team and 3 dependency teams, each with 1 engineer and 1 TPM
Duration
10 months
Affected
Anti-Cheat, Moderation, Matchmaking, Player Support, Live Operations

The problem

Player bans were built into one big, tangled system. They ranged from limiting a player to matches against computer opponents to banning an account for a set period or for good. Five teams relied on that system, and any change by one team risked breaking the others’ work. Moderation had outgrown it and couldn’t scale on top of it.

Banning a player was also not self-service. Anyone who needed a ban had to go through the engineering teams that owned the banning services and wait for them to do it.

The old system was called Feature Bans, but it had grown far past banning. Teams were using it to store all kinds of per-player settings, so the name had become misleading. It also had no record of who changed what, little tracking of how it was used, no way to sort settings into categories, and weak tools for the people running it.

Activision was breaking its big system into smaller, independent services. This project would move that player state into its own service, called Player Flags, that other teams could connect to easily. The goal was a service for any rule attached to a player, not just a moderation tool.

How it fits together

Other systems, such as Anti-Cheat, Moderation and Player Support tools, ask the service to put a flag on a player. The service stores it, then sends a notice to whatever needs to act on it: the player’s game app, or the game servers and matchmaking that put players into matches. Flags were used for cheat prevention, moderation limits, matchmaking behavior, game server behavior, trust scores, feature switches, event groups and support cases.

A simple way to say it: Moderation decides what should happen. Player Flags stores that decision and delivers it to everything that has to enforce it.

What made it hard

A first working version had to be ready by the next game’s launch date, and that date would not move. Five teams with different needs all depended on the same service, so every requirement had to work for all of them. The build also depended on four other teams, a UI team and three dependency teams, each with its own engineer and TPM. That made 13 people across five teams to keep in step.

Key decisions

Hold large batch actions until after launch

Options
Build everything the teams asked for in the first version, or ship the essentials and add the rest later.
The call
I kept the essentials and moved large batch actions, which ban up to tens of thousands of players at once, to after launch.
Why
The first version had to be ready for the game’s launch date. At launch we expected one-off cheaters, not large groups, so banning in batches wasn’t critical on day one. The cost was that Anti-Cheat stayed on their old workflow for a while. It wasn’t self-service, it depended on the team that owned banning inside the old system, and it took more manual steps.
Pushback
Anti-Cheat wanted batch actions at launch and pushed back at first. We proposed a date for adding them in a later season, about 4 weeks after launch, and they agreed to use their existing workflow until then. The engineers supported the cut because it gave them more time and cushion for the first version.
Proof
When batch actions were added later, one large job slowed down urgent notices in production. That confirmed holding them back was the right call, and it led to the redesign described below.

Keep the team on the first version

Options
Let design work continue on problems outside the first version, or bring it back to the agreed requirements.
The call
During the design phase, one of our engineers began working on problems he had spotted that were outside the first version’s requirements. I steered the work back to the plan.
Why
It was using up engineering time and delaying the point where we could present our requirements and design to the customer teams for approval. That put the launch date at risk.
Pushback
He stood his ground at first and wanted to address those problems from the start. We agreed to accept them as risks because the chance of them turning into real issues was low. The trade-off was more refactoring work later, which was worth it to stay on track.
Proof
The team finished early enough to start the next phase ahead of schedule.

How it ran

  1. 1 monthDiscoveryInterviewed the five teams to learn how they worked and what frustrated them.
  2. 1.5 monthsRequirements and designTurned what I heard into a clear list of requirements and cut scope to fit the date.
  3. 1 monthAlignmentGot engineering and all five teams to agree on who owned what, who depended on whom, and how we’d test together.
  4. 6.5 monthsBuild, load test, launchBuilt the first version, tested it under peak player numbers, and shipped for launch.

Load testing took longer than expected. We adjusted the schedule and carved out more time to bring the service’s load down to a reasonable level at peak player numbers.

After launch, the service became a critical path for Anti-Cheat, game security and other enforcement work. The notice sender had been built for “best effort” delivery, with every update waiting in one line no matter its size or urgency. When a large batch job ran, it held up urgent notices. The write-up of that incident led the engineers to split the work in two. Small updates keep a fast path. Large jobs go through an upload: a team sends a compressed file, the service processes it in the background with its own workers, and urgent notices are never delayed behind it.

Outcome

  • Shipped on time for the game’s launch.
  • Banning became self-service. Anti-Cheat and other stakeholders with the right permissions could ban players themselves through a new interface, without relying on engineering teams.
  • Finished early enough to start the next phase ahead of schedule.
  • The five teams gave positive feedback on the new service.
  • Delivered a real step in Activision’s move to smaller, independent services.
  • The service grew from a ban replacement into the shared place where player restrictions are stored and delivered for several teams.
  • Batch actions, once added, exposed a delay for urgent notices. The team fixed it with a separate background path for large jobs.

What I’d do differently

I would write down what is out of scope at design kickoff. A short list of what the first version will not include, with a place to park concerns for later, would have caught the design detour sooner.

I would also bring the batch actions trade-off to Anti-Cheat earlier, with the date for adding them already attached. That would likely have avoided the initial pushback.

I run my own job search like a project. A written process and a few small tested programs find openings, tailor a one-page resume and keep one tracking sheet up to date.

How it works today

I say “run my workflow for new jobs” and it runs from search to finished resumes. If I paste a single job post, it skips the search steps. I still open each application, attach the resume and submit it myself.

Starts it Program or search Claude decides Google Drive and Sheets Result

How it is built

I manage Claude the way I would manage a team. The instructions are written down, Claude makes the calls and small tested programs do the repeat work.

  • InstructionsA plain-language document that says what to do, what can go wrong and what is never allowed.
  • DecisionsClaude reads it, picks the order, handles surprises and asks me when it is unsure.
  • ProgramsScripts do the repeat work: searching, building the PDF, saving to Drive and updating the sheet.
  • When it breaksI fix the program, test it again and rewrite the instructions so it does not fail the same way twice.
